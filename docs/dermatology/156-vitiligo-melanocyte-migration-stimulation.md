## 2. Pathophysiology of Vitiligo & The Limitations of Legacy Treatments

### 2.1 The Cellular Microenvironment in Vitiligo
In vitiligo lesions, epidermal melanocytes are destroyed via CD8+ T-cell-mediated autoimmune attacks, driven by the interferon-gamma (IFN-$\gamma$)–CXCL9/CXCL10 chemokine axis. However, the hair follicle bulge remains an immune-privileged niche. Within this reservoir, undifferentiated melanocyte stem cells (McSCs) survive the autoimmune onslaught. 

Under physiological conditions, these McSCs are highly restricted in their migration. Traditional therapies—such as narrowband ultraviolet B (NB-UVB) phototherapy, topical corticosteroids, and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (e.g., ruxolitinib)—attempt to suppress local inflammation and weakly stimulate perifollicular repigmentation. 

### 2.2 The Failure of Legacy Interventions
Legacy treatments are fundamentally flawed:
1. **Incomplete Efficacy:** Phototherapy relies on stochastic, passive diffusion of melanocytes, requiring 30 to 100 sessions over 6–12 months, with a high rate of relapse once therapy ceases.
2. **Severe Side Effects:** Chronic topical corticosteroid use causes skin atrophy, telangiectasia, and systemic absorption. JAK inhibitors carry black-box warnings for serious infections, malignancies, and major adverse cardiovascular events.
3. **Symptom Management vs. Cure:** None of these therapies address the underlying biophysical guidance cues required to actively mobilize and guide McSCs across the basement membrane into depigmented zones.
