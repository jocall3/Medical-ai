## 2. Neurobiological Foundations of ADHD

### 2.1 Prefrontal Cortex Hypoactivity
Functional neuroimaging consistently demonstrates that individuals diagnosed with ADHD exhibit marked hypoactivity and reduced gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex, particularly within the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC). These regions are the anatomical substrates for executive functions, including:
*   **Working Memory:** The retention and manipulation of transient information.
*   **Cognitive Flexibility:** The ability to transition between disparate tasks or cognitive frameworks.
*   **Inhibitory Control:** The suppression of prepotent, inappropriate behavioral responses.

In a healthy brain, the DLPFC exerts top-down inhibitory control over subcortical structures, such as the striatum and amygdala. In the ADHD brain, this top-down regulation is severely compromised, leading to the classic clinical presentation of inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity.

### 2.2 Network Dysregulation: DMN vs. CEN
Beyond localized hypoactivity, ADHD is fundamentally a disorder of large-scale brain network dynamics. Specifically, it involves a failure of the toggle mechanism between two major networks:
1.  **The Default Mode Network (DMN):** Associated with internal mentation, daydreaming, and self-referential thought. The DMN is centered in the medial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex.
2.  **The Central Executive Network (CEN):** Associated with externally focused, goal-directed attention. The CEN is anchored in the DLPFC and posterior parietal cortex.

In neurotypical individuals, these networks are anti-correlated: when the CEN activates to perform a task, the DMN is suppressed. In individuals with ADHD, this anti-correlation is degraded. The DMN fails to deactivate during task performance, intruding upon the CEN and causing spontaneous lapses in attention, cognitive fatigue, and distractibility.

### 2.3 Neurotransmitter Dynamics
Traditional psychiatry attributes ADHD to a simple deficiency in dopamine (DA) and norepinephrine (NE) within the synaptic clefts of the prefrontal cortex. While catecholaminergic signaling is indeed altered, the traditional solution—flooding the entire brain with exogenous stimulants—is a crude, non-specific intervention that causes widespread receptor downregulation, cardiovascular strain, and psychological dependence. The AI Hospital's approach focuses instead on restoring endogenous neurotransmitter synthesis and receptor sensitivity through localized, activity-dependent neuroplasticity.
